\section{Scope and reading guide}
This supplement accompanies the main manuscript. It records exact execution conventions, diagnostic controls, resource accounting, and the relationship between historical and accepted experiments. The original research plan is preserved as \code{master_design.tex}; it is a plan, not evidence that every proposed phase was completed. The research repository preserves completed, failed, interrupted, and unexecuted protocols separately. Only completed and audited observations support the numerical claims below.

There are three evidence levels: ideal independent-count simulation at full-model scale; direct official THRML sampling at operator scale; and physical devices. The third level is not part of this study. The term K1 counts one logical programmable stochastic element per scalar. It does not mean one bit of total storage, a physical allocation on a chip, or deterministic one-bit neural arithmetic.

\section{Finite encoder and reconstruction details}
\subsection{Grouping and constant groups}
The encoder accepts finite FP32-representable weights. A matrix can be flattened in storage order or transposed before flattening; the inverse permutation is applied after reconstruction. Groups are contiguous after that permutation, including a possibly shorter final group. A group stores its FP32 minimum and FP32 span, and one 12-bit command per scalar in a uint16 array. A zero span means the reconstructed group is exactly constant irrespective of the sampled state.

The probability field is clipped to $[-4,4]$ and rounded to 4,096 equally spaced command levels. Decoding evaluates the field in FP32 and the logistic probability in FP64. This exact order is important for hash replay. The supported group sizes in the SDK range from one through 65,536; the reported model configurations use 7, 16, or 128 as specified, not a per-parameter range that hides an extra full-precision copy.

\subsection{Independent counts and random keys}
Reconstruction calls NumPy's binomial generator once per scalar instead of emitting $T$ explicit Bernoulli bits. Each canonical tensor uses a random stream initialized from the experimental seed and tensor index. Groups are processed in deterministic chunks aligned to their boundaries. Counts use uint16 through $T=65{,}535$, and uint32 above that threshold. A SHA-256 digest over counts and a digest over reconstructed BF16 values are preserved in per-tensor records.

A binomial draw is an exact distributional shortcut only for independent identically distributed observations. It does not model temporal or cross-device correlation. The native and nonideal experiments are separate. Python random-number generation time also cannot be interpreted as a physical device's sampling time.

\subsection{API example}
The standalone package can reconstruct a finite-control program without model checkpoints:
\begin{verbatim}
import numpy as np
from pbit1 import FinitePBitProgram

w = np.array([[0.1, -0.2], [0.6, 0.3]], dtype=np.float32)
program = FinitePBitProgram.encode(w, group_size=2)
sample = program.reconstruct(32768, seed=7)
print(sample.weights)
print(program.storage())
\end{verbatim}
The return value is FP32. The research pipeline applies its explicit BF16 conversion afterward. The SDK's storage report counts array payload, not Python-object overhead, a reconstructed matrix, a retained reference model, or external hardware.

\subsection{Information bound}
For $T$ independent Bernoulli observations with interior probability $p$, the Fisher information is $\mathcal I(p)=T/[p(1-p)]$. The sample mean attains the Cram\'er--Rao variance bound $p(1-p)/T$ for unbiased estimation of $p$. Under a fixed affine range with span $d$, weight variance is $d^2p(1-p)/T$. This quantifies an estimation limit; it does not prove that a neural task needs that much parameterwise accuracy. Finite probability controls add a separate bias term that does not vanish with $T$.

\section{Exact sampling-allocation procedure}
The gradient proxy averages $\sum_i (\partial\mathcal L/\partial w_i)^2v_i$ over eight short training windows, where $v_i$ is the single-sample variance. Candidate one-dimensional ranges use group sizes 16, 32, 64, and 128. Matrix candidates retain group size 128 with the two flattening orientations. A second group of eight calibration windows evaluates proposals before freezing the complete GPU test.

The executed discrete allocation starts each tensor at $T_i=1{,}024$. Let $S_i$ be its selected gradient-weighted variance coefficient and $N_i$ its size. Doubling $T_i$ reduces proxy variance by $S_i/(2T_i)$ at an added cost of $N_iT_i$ observations. The greedy ratio is therefore
\begin{equation}
 \frac{S_i}{2N_iT_i^2}.
\end{equation}
The algorithm repeatedly doubles the eligible tensor with the largest ratio until it meets the recorded proxy target or reaches the cap $T_i\leq32{,}768$. The final accepted policy is \code{gradient_proxy_directions_vectorG16}. This is a fixed per-tensor schedule selected offline, not confidence-based early stopping for individual examples. The original plan's adaptive stopping and probability-aware fine-tuning remain separate possible extensions.

The full evaluation uses five fresh seeds, 2026111401 through 2026111405. Its frozen acceptance conditions require complete target coverage, all learned parameters K1, maximum sample count 32,768, nominal cost at most 60\% of uniform, and upper PPL-ratio interval at most 1.02. The observed cost is 58.324\%, with upper interval 1.01660. The BF16 reference arrays match the earlier complete baseline.

\subsection{Signal scale and neuron structure}
For each LFM3 block, the baseline activation study uses 8,224 positions and 2,048 channels. The activation energy participation ratio is $(\sum_j e_j)^2/\sum_j e_j^2$, where $e_j$ is the channel second moment. Ratios range approximately from 9 to 654 across blocks. This measures concentration of activation energy, not the number of removable neurons.

\section{Covariance estimator and interpretation}
Let $e_{s,t}\in\mathbb R^{64}$ denote the output error for seed $s$ at fixed position $t$, relative to the deterministic baseline, and let $\bar e_t$ be its mean across $S=5$ seeds. The estimator is
\begin{equation}
 \widehat C=\frac1M\sum_{t=1}^{M}\frac1{S-1}
 \sum_{s=1}^{S}(e_{s,t}-\bar e_t)(e_{s,t}-\bar e_t)^\top,
 \qquad M=8{,}224.
\end{equation}
It averages conditional covariance over input positions. It is not obtained by pooling positions and subtracting one global mean. Sufficient statistics include cross sums, the mean outer-product contribution, channel indices, and the resulting matrices. The independent auditor checks all 48 matrices and a direct small-array covariance fixture. All five model draws replay all 8,192 training target losses bitwise before covariance accumulation.

The participation ratio for covariance is $(\operatorname{tr}C)^2/\operatorname{tr}(C^2)$; the leading-eigenvalue fraction is $\lambda_{\max}(C)/\operatorname{tr}C$. The high-error channels were selected using this same training cohort. Their more concentrated covariance therefore cannot be interpreted as an unbiased population comparison. Averaging conditional covariances may have rank greater than $S-1$ because input positions have different error subspaces.

\subsection{Correlation, drift, and calibration}
Operator-level moment calculations and simulated calibration probes examine departures from ideal independent sampling; these nonideal models were not executed through native THRML. A stationary binary process with lag correlation $\rho^k$ has finite-sample variance inflation
\begin{equation}
 1+2\sum_{k=1}^{T-1}(1-k/T)\rho^k,
\end{equation}
which approaches $(1+\rho)/(1-\rho)$ for large $T$. Accordingly, the common approximation $T_{\rm eff}\approx T(1-\rho)/(1+\rho)$ has an explicit finite-$T$ limitation. Cross-device correlation adds covariance terms to output variance and must not be represented solely by a scalar effective sample size.

The preserved head investigations include 900 nonideality cases, and finite-probe, prior-aware calibration analyses with 140 cases and 48 optimizer checks. Their calibration priors are assumptions, not measured device populations. An unregularized calibration fit can worsen estimation; an appropriate prior can help in the tested simulator. Probe counts and calibration storage belong in the cost ledger. Drift sensitivity is simulated; no hardware recalibration interval is established.

The full GPT-2 gain/offset experiment uses 16 CPU training cases: one baseline and five seeds each for ideal, mismatched, and oracle-corrected conditions. It audits the matched device arrays and nine parameter replays. Oracle coefficients are known exactly, then re-quantized to the finite control grid. This separates programming bias from sampling variance but does not solve the physical calibration problem.

\subsection{Earlier experiments and failures}
The earlier adapted LFM3 checkpoint study used 2,048 fixed validation targets and a same-fit BF16 reference of PPL 75.3147. Its T1024 cohort gave PPL 83.9167 and missed the 2\% criterion; T4096 gave 76.1024 and also missed the criterion because its upper interval exceeded the margin. T8192 gave 75.1547, ratio 0.99788 with interval [0.98062, 1.01544], and passed. These adapted, shorter-task results are not pooled with the final original-checkpoint evaluation.

An earlier GPT-2 T8192 cohort failed, whereas DistilBERT passed its original criterion. The local range diagnosis in the main paper investigates this variation. An additional 10,240-target LFM3 cloud study was interrupted and has no accepted final quality outcome. A GB10/H100 bridge reproduced stochastic and nominal arrays, while its BF16 reference shifted approximately 0.00515\% in perplexity. The initial strict bitwise-control failure is preserved, together with the later documented hardware-specific-reference amendment. These observations are historical context, not additional independent proof of the final Spark claims.

The archive also preserves failed implementation and audit attempts, including calibration bookkeeping errors and revised auditors. Only the corrected completed runs are used for accepted statistics, and exact executed source snapshots remain available. Repeated audits of the same data are not counted as new experimental replications.

Ideal packed controls are 12 bits per scalar, but the current NumPy implementation stores them in uint16. No packed 12-bit runtime is claimed. Fixed shape, tensor addressing, layout flags, per-tensor sample budgets, and Python bookkeeping incur further overhead. There is no residual side table in the accepted K1 model configurations and no excluded deterministic learned subset. Diagnostic restoration controls explicitly relax that condition.

R1 reconstructs weights once for repeated inference. This preserves a decoded weight array. Discarding the original model after encoding can remove a reference copy, but not the working weights needed by this implementation. Device density, bit storage, free random transitions, all-node observations, and elapsed latency are separate metrics. The original four-element word baseline and the stronger coarse/fractional baseline differ in stored control information; neither licenses a blanket systems-efficiency statement.

The package and bounded archive preserve source snapshots indexed by SHA-256, protocols, summaries, independent audits, package versions, and omitted-asset hashes. The manuscript's \code{EVIDENCE_MANIFEST.json} maps each bundled figure/input to its originating artifact. Manuscript plots derived from JSON can be regenerated without model weights; raw-output auditors require the omitted model assets and arrays or complete reruns. A hash manifest verifies integrity, not scientific correctness by itself.

For model reproduction, first restore the pinned checkpoints and dataset layouts under their original licenses, recreate the recorded runtime, and use a new output directory. Producers preserve original protocols and typically reject conflicting or already-failed state. Do not edit executed producer files to force old results to pass. Large-model replays, native samplers, and CPU diagnostics use different runtime environments as recorded. Representative module commands are documented in the repository reproduction guide.

The original broad design additionally proposes extensive image-model sweeps, expectation-aware kernels, online adaptive stopping, stochastic training, mixed spatial/temporal optimization, and physical hardware characterization. They are not all established by this manuscript. The final evidence addresses frozen-model feasibility, failure mechanisms, finite-control limits, and selected repairs. General deployment claims would require new untouched tasks, optimized quantization comparisons, long-context/generation evaluations, and measured device programming, retention, correlation, readout, and accumulation costs.
